# 5.3 Preclinical safety data

General toxicology

No general toxicology studies have been conducted with colchicine, however, no specific safety pharmacology concerns have been identified beyond those attributable to the known pharmacodynamic properties of colchicine.
Any overdose with colchicine is potentially lethal.
In repeated-dose toxicity studies, dose-dependent adverse reactions were observed mainly in rapidly proliferating tissues, including the gastrointestinal tract, bone marrow, lymphoid tissue, and reproductive organs, with species-specific sensitivity reported.

Carcinogenicity

Based on the results of two-year studies in mice and rats, colchicine did not demonstrate any evidence of carcinogenic potential at oral doses up to 3 mg/kg/day in mice and 2 mg/kg/day in rats, corresponding to exposures approximately 29- and 38-times greater than the recommended human dose of 0.5 mg on a mg/m² basis.

Genotoxicity

Colchicine was negative for mutagenicity in the bacterial reverse mutation assay.
In a chromosomal aberration assay in cultured human white blood cells, colchicine treatment resulted in the formation of micronuclei.
Since published studies demonstrated that colchicine induces aneuploidy from the process of mitotic nondisjunction without structural DNA changes, colchicine is not considered clastogenic, although micronuclei are formed.

Reproductive and developmental toxicity

Published nonclinical studies demonstrated that colchicine-induced disruption of microtubule formation affects meiosis and mitosis.
Fertility studies also reported abnormal sperm morphology and reduced sperm counts in males, and interference with sperm penetration, second meiotic division and normal cleavage in females when exposed to colchicine.
Moreover, published animal studies indicate that colchicine causes embryofetal toxicity and altered postnatal development at exposures within or above the clinical therapeutic range.

Colchicine interferes with microtubule formation and mitotic spindle function, resulting in inhibition of cell division.
In animal studies, colchicine was embryotoxic and teratogenic, and induced aneuploidy, chromosomal abnormalities, and impaired embryonic development.
Adverse effects on fertility were observed in both male and female animals, including impaired spermatogenesis, oocyte maturation, and implantation.
These effects were dose-dependent and related to the timing of exposure.
In humans, cases of reversible oligospermia and azoospermia have been reported during colchicine therapy.
A causal relationship has not been definitively established.

Although these findings raise a theoretical concern for reproductive toxicity in humans, extensive clinical experience with colchicine at therapeutic doses has not demonstrated a consistent signal of increased risk of congenital malformations or permanent infertility.
Nevertheless, due to the mechanism of action and non-clinical findings, precautionary contraceptive measures are recommended (see section 4.6).
