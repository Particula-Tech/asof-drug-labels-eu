# 4.6 Fertility, pregnancy and lactation

Women of childbearing potential / Contraception in males and females

Based on the mechanism of action of colchicine, which interferes with microtubule polymerisation and cell division, colchicine has the potential to adversely affect gametogenesis (see section 5.3).
Women of childbearing potential should use effective contraception during treatment with colchicine and for at least one month after discontinuation of treatment.
Male patients should not father a child during treatment with colchicine and for at least three months after discontinuation of treatment.

Pregnancy

Animal studies have shown that colchicine is embryotoxic and teratogenic, consistent with its pharmacological activity, including effects on chromosome segregation and cell division (see section 5.3).

Available human data from the literature, mainly derived from long-term use of colchicine in familial Mediterranean fever and other inflammatory conditions, have not identified an increased risk of major congenital malformations, miscarriage, or adverse pregnancy outcomes when colchicine is used at therapeutic doses.
However, data in pregnant women with ischaemic cardiovascular disease are limited.
As a precautionary measure, colchicine should not be used during pregnancy unless the clinical condition of the woman requires treatment.

Breast-feeding

Colchicine is excreted into human milk.
The breastfed infant may receive up to approximately 10% of the maternal weight-adjusted dose.
Available published data have not reported adverse reactions in breastfed infants exposed to colchicine.
However, given the pharmacological activity of colchicine, a risk to the breastfed infant cannot be excluded.

A decision must be made whether to discontinue breast-feeding or to discontinue/abstain from colchicine therapy, taking into account the benefit of breast-feeding for the child and the benefit of therapy for the woman.

Fertility
Animal studies have demonstrated that colchicine can impair fertility in both males and females, including effects on spermatogenesis and oogenesis (see section 5.3).
